# Frameworks: Kunal Shah

Built from 10 video sources (S001 to S010), 2016 to 2026. Most-used first. Scoring lines are guidance drawn from his stated thresholds, not his words. He names no books, so none of these come from one.

## 1. Delta 4 (efficiency delta)
- What: Score the old way and the new product out of 10 on efficiency. A gap of 4 or more unlocks three things: the switch is irreversible, users tolerate failures, and users brag about it. Example: cab 3, Uber 9.
- Steps: (1) name the behaviour people already do, (2) score the old way, (3) score the new product, (4) subtract, (5) have blunt outsiders check your score.
- Use when: judging any product, feature or business, especially whether it beats today's jugaad.
- Scoring (guidance): gap of 4 or more, build. Below 4, he predicts reversible adoption, and discounts only erode value.
- Quote: "every time you see that the product efficiency delta is greater than or equal to four, three things happen. It is irreversible." (S001@06:52)
- Quote: "if you don't have a startup that is Delta 4 you are very it's going to be very hard to create wealth" (S010@08:57)
- Sources: 5 (S001, S002, S005, S007, S010). Oldest statement is S010 (2016), where he calls it "work in progress" (S010). Newest is S007 (2026): "Just build something that people care about and make it significantly better, cheaper, and faster." (S007@59:36)
- Caveat: the score is self-assessed and rough. Delta 1 to 3 are not defined in these sources; "Delta 1 category" appears only in S010 on discounts.

## 2. The suit test (below 4 stays reversible)
- What: A case where tech did not beat the old way. Buying a suit online scored 5 against 7 offline, so no irreversible switch.
- Use when: someone says "we put it on an app" and expects people to switch.
- Quote: "when the delta is less than four, what you will discover is that it is reversible. You will not see people bragging about it and you have zero tolerance for it." (S001@08:56)
- Quote: "just by adding tech it doesn't become delta four right it has to improve the efficiency score of the desired behavior" (S002@54:47)
- Sources: 2 (S001, S002).

## 3. UBP, unique brag-worthy proposition
- What: A Delta 4 product makes users talk about it, so acquisition cost falls toward zero. If nobody brags, suspect the delta is low.
- Use when: judging CAC and word of mouth.
- Quote: "Every time humans unlock a Delta 4 product or service, they cannot stop talking or sharing about it." (S001@07:22)
- Quote: "any product that has Delta 4 has unique brag worthy proposition" (S010@08:07)
- Sources: 3 (S001, S002, S010).

## 4. The jugaad test (the incumbent workaround is the competitor)
- What: The real rival is the cheap existing workaround, not another app. A maid beats a laundry app in India, so about 800 laundry startups copied from the West found no gain.
- Steps: (1) find what Indians already do today, (2) price its true effort, (3) ask whether your product is 4 points better than that, not better than the Western original.
- Quote: "the more efficient way of getting your laundry is is getting a maid that is Delta 4 not going to a laundry app" (S010@11:36)
- Quote: "in india we had maids coming at home why do we need a laundry startup" (S002@50:37)
- Sources: 3 (S002, S010, S003). S003 applies it to weddings: "people are not seeking efficiency sometimes" (S003@52:16). Inferred from S003 that where the process is the point, efficiency tools miss.

## 5. Six limits of Delta 4
- What: Reasons a high-delta product still fails: affordability, readiness (smartphones), learning effort, micro-configurations (the maid), collective participation (WhatsApp), and branded lanes where the state change becomes a verb (Google).
- Use when: the score looks good but adoption stalls.
- Quote: "if I create an Uber like app and nobody has got smartphones in the this country will it work no it will not" (S010@13:41)
- Quote: "it is hard to create a better product and still move people right" (S010@14:55)
- Sources: 1 (S010).

## 6. Hacking Delta 4 and the prototype test
- What: Brands can fake the score through influencers, piggybacking on another Delta 4, or faking inefficiencies. To check the real one, show an uncoded prototype or slide to stakeholders and consumers and build only when they are excited.
- Quote: "only when everybody feels excited about it you start building that" (S010@24:35)
- Quote: "you can fake Delta 4 by the power of marketing and branding" (S010@17:10)
- Quote: "in India we are factories of sugar coating" (S010@22:38)
- Sources: 1 (S010). He says ask blunt founders, not family, for the score.

## 7. Rivers and dams
- What: A river is an existing human motivation. The product is the dam. Wealth needs a good dam on a real river, and tech founders keep polishing dams where no river flows.
- Steps: (1) name the motivation, (2) test the price ladder: lakhs, 10,000 rupees, 1 rupee, 5 rupees, or only free, (3) then improve the dam.
- Use when: judging whether demand exists before judging the product.
- Scoring (guidance): if buyers will only pay free or 1 rupee, the river is thin.
- Quote: "Most tech founders are great at building dams and terrible at finding rivers." (S007@21:55)
- Quote: "Rivers don't come. Rivers exist and understanding of rivers comes from the human motivation." (S007@00:53)
- Sources: 4 (S002, S006, S007, S008). S008 states it as motivation times efficiency: "you can make something efficient that nobody cares about" (S008@41:22).

## 8. Status and gross margin (utility versus vanity)
- What: Gross margin exists where a product lets people jump or signal social status, and pure utility drifts to zero margin. Living-room products earned 3x the margin of bedroom ones.
- Use when: pricing, margin, and choosing between a utility and a status play.
- Quote: "gross margins exist when you allow human beings to jump their social status and gross margins disappear when you do not help them increase their social status" (S002@24:07)
- Quote: "products that increase your status will always be higher gross margin and you can either increase the status or prevent status loss" (S004@62:15)
- Sources: 6 (S001, S002, S003, S004, S006, S007).

## 9. Value of time (India does not price it)
- What: Without hourly pay, Indians rarely price their time, so time-saving products struggle to monetise. Applies to SaaS, convenience and productivity apps.
- Use when: the pitch is "saves you time".
- Quote: "products that sell convenience and save you your time rarely monetize" (S002@17:49)
- Quote: "it's very hard to build SaaS for India because you don't value time." (S007@26:21)
- Sources: 7 (S001, S002, S003, S004, S005, S007, S008).
- Shift: in S004 (2020) and S002 (2022) he says time products are hard to monetise. In S008 (2025) he treats it as a national condition to fix: "Prosperity comes when we respect time." (S008@19:21) Newer reading: a cultural gap he wants closed, not only a market limit.

## 10. Top 25 million (India 1 cohort) and slices
- What: Per capita income is concentrated, so build for the small group that has money and values time, not a 100 million average. Widen cohort by cohort. This is his version of India 1 versus the rest, without the label.
- Use when: sizing an Indian consumer market and picking a first customer.
- Quote: "the value of time and per capita income is concentrated with 25 million families and therefore focusing on them is important." (S001@33:47)
- Quote: "If you make it work for 100 200 million, if their life changes, the next 200 million is ready." (S008@16:06)
- Sources: 5 (S001, S002, S003, S008, S009).
- Changed view: in 2021 (S009) he says the market lacks depth for a big company from home customers. In 2024 (S001) he calls India "the most promising market that one can be building for right now". Newer is S001.
- Gap: India 1/2/3 as named labels, no recorded position. S008 shows he does not use the terms.

## 11. Who pays: the family test
- What: Ask what people want and whether they will pay. Pitch it to parents and cousins and ask if they would pay.
- Quote: "what do people want and will they pay money for it" (S004@03:52)
- Quote: "your mom pay for this will your cfo pay for this will your kid pay for this" (S002@65:53)
- Sources: 3 (S002, S004, S007). In S010 (2016) he warns family flatter you, so pair it with blunt outsiders.

## 12. Ratchet effect (no going back)
- What: After a higher-quality experience, people do not return. His five-star hotel example.
- Use when: judging whether a switch will stick.
- Quote: "once you experience something high quality it's very hard to reverse." (S007@45:40)
- Sources: 1 (S007). Inferred from S001, S007, S010 that it is the same idea as Delta 4 irreversibility.

## 13. Boring versus interesting transactions
- What: People want less time and money on boring transactions (bills, fuel, recharge) and will spend on interesting ones (holidays, weddings). Build to remove the boring ones.
- Quote: "There are boring transactions and are interesting transactions in life." (S008@22:10)
- Quote: "trying to reduce time and money spent on all boring things and invest in all the transactions that are interesting" (S006@16:07)
- Sources: 2 (S006, S008).

## 14. Concentration of trust
- What: In low-trust markets a few trusted names launch many unrelated products, which explains super apps and Tata. Newcomers need borrowed trust.
- Quote: "tata group is this concentration of trust and they can launch a car to a refrigerator to salt to furniture" (S002@29:31)
- Quote: "The joy of trying new things is not so high in low trust nations." (S001@29:30)
- Sources: 3 (S001, S002, S004).

## 15. Swiss knife after the knife
- What: Be one thing for someone before everything for everyone. With low revenue per user, he also says one app has to do many things later.
- Quote: "becoming everything for everyone is the fastest way to die" (S009@43:15)
- Quote: "they believe that they have to build a swiss knife when the consumer only was looking for a knife" (S002@64:50)
- Sources: 3 (S001, S002, S009).
- Tension: S002 says "focus is almost a curse in asian markets" (S002@34:10) while S009 says focus on a cohort. Inferred from S001, S002, S009: start narrow, then cross-sell once trust exists.

## 16. Incentive over punishment (behaviour change)
- What: To change behaviour you add an incentive. Removing friction alone does not change motivation.
- Quote: "whenever we are trying to change behavior we have to always put incentive to change the behavior" (S006@18:35)
- Quote: "just making things accessible does not change the core motivation." (S007@58:57)
- Sources: 2 (S006, S007).

## 17. The dinner test (founder pitch filter)
- What: Explain the idea without jargon, as a user would to a friend. If it cannot be passed on over dinner, it will not spread and CAC stays high. Also try Hindi, and ask about real motivation versus stated function.
- Quote: "if you cannot distill your idea to a transmissible conversation at dinner it's not going to spread" (S002@63:08)
- Sources: 1 (S002). He says about 90 percent of founders fail it.

## 18. Second-order thinking and thought experiments
- What: Push an extreme scenario and follow the chain of effects. Used to find second-order effects and to test people.
- Quote: "The brain absolutely hates second order thinking unless you've trained your brain" (S001@73:44)
- Quote: "doing thought experiments is the smartest way of of becoming sharper in life" (S005@55:10)
- Sources: 3 (S001, S003, S005).
