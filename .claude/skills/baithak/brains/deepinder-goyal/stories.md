# Stories: Deepinder Goyal

Built from the extract notes in build/deepinder-goyal/notes/ (batch 01 and batch 02). These are the cases he reuses across interviews, or single cases with a clear business lesson. Stories are paraphrased. No quotation marks are used here, so nothing here is his exact wording. Source ids are in brackets.

## Ten countries, half failed

Dubai broke even in about six months, which gave false confidence. Then ten countries launched in about six months and half of them failed. He says some US attempts also did not work and the company pulled out. Lesson: one playbook does not fit every market, so localise by market and do not read one win as proof (S002, S004).

## Buy the local leader

A two-minute call with a local leader in New Zealand led to an acquisition that paid back within about 18 months. Early targets had low valuations, about 1 to 2 million dollars each, and the company moved all traffic onto Zomato after buying. Lesson: buy the local leader when it is available and cheap (S002).

## Urban Spoon and the rival's home market

The company bought Urban Spoon for Canada and Australia, not the US, after a scout trip. Lesson: do not attack a rival in its home market (S002).

## Food eBay rename

A legal notice from eBay arrived five days before the planned name switch. An investor had earlier pointed out the risk. Lesson: switch early when a bigger rival may come after you (S002, S006).

## Valuation refused on trust

In 2012 an investor offered a lower valuation (3 million on 25 instead of 30 pre-money). He refused, because he could not trust that investor. Lesson: trust matters more than a small valuation gap (S002).

## The fast yes

A first email was archived as a sales pitch. A reply sent around two in the morning led to a meeting the next morning. Later, a term sheet on a one-third stake was decided in about seven or eight minutes, once the offer and due diligence were right. Lessons: answer the people who matter fast, and decide fast when the deal is right (S002, S003, S006).

## Empty bank, investors in control

A founder with no cash and about 40 percent of the company owned by investors. Lesson: do not spend before the money arrives (S002).

## Four months of cash

The company kept four months of burn in the bank. Each month it cut burn so the balance still covered four months after that month's spend. He credits this rule for getting through near-zero cash phases. Lesson: keep a fixed cushion and cut burn to keep it (S004).

## Sold the UAE business to keep going

When a rival raised about a billion dollars and Zomato had little in the bank, the company sold its UAE business for about 170 million dollars and kept working. Lesson: solve the problem with the resources you have, on a short timeline (S004).

## Expansion abroad, 2013 to 2015

Zomato expanded abroad from 2013 to 2015 and closed those markets when India was about 93 percent of revenue. He says he should have focused on India. Lesson: stay in the core market until it is secure (S004).

## Hiring a head of marketing from outside

An outside head of marketing takes about six months to start and about a year before results show. Growing an insider takes about three months. Lesson: promote from within when someone can grow into the role (S004).

## The culture handbook

He wrote a handbook for employees. It became a roadmap for how people think, behave and grow, and new hires read it several times. Lesson: write down the principles so people can use them to decide (S004).

## The chill experiment

He stepped back for a few months, then saw the organisation would end if he kept doing it, and went back to being first in and last out. Lesson: a leader cannot chill (S003).

## COVID: 90 percent of business lost

The company lost about 90 percent of its business. It raised cash and put in its own money to feed the team and their families. Lesson: serve your own team first in a crisis (S003).

## Post-IPO narrative flip

The public story turned within about ten days of listing, and the company became "evil" in public perception. Lesson: do not react to the stock; let the work speak (S007).

## Layoffs in 2015 and after COVID

The company laid off staff in 2015 and again after COVID. That experience shapes how it keeps fixed cost low, and why it does not want to lay off again (S007).

## Nagpur and small cities

Nagpur was a city around the 30th to 35th rank. 78 percent of users there had never ordered food from an app before. A city of about 150,000 people produced about 4,000 orders a day. Lesson: the market is bigger than the top 20 cities (S008).

## Dinner peak and turning orders away

Most orders land between 8:20 and 8:35 pm. On rainy days orders spike while riders cannot ride. Serving the last few peak orders would add about 30 percent to cost, so the company turns orders away and tells customers upfront. Lesson: plan for peaks and turn away orders you cannot serve (S008).

## Per-order rupee walk

On a delivery with an average order of about Rs 400, the blended commission is about Rs 80, delivery cost is Rs 50 to 70 and the customer pays Rs 20 to 25. Net profit is about Rs 5 to 10 per order at scale and close to Rs 1 or 2 across the country. Lesson: break every order into rupees before you judge a market (S004).

## Rider pay and training

The company does not cut pay for lateness. It trains riders gradually to finish one order before starting another. Lesson: train late riders rather than punish them (S007).
