# Stories: Sridhar Vembu

19 stories and cases he reuses, with the lesson he draws from each. Built from S001, S003 to S012 (S002 is excluded). Source ids in parentheses.

1. **Six engineers in Tenkasi.** Around 2012, six engineers went to Tenkasi. The first year was slow and lonely. After two to three years there were 20 to 30 people. Lesson: keep a small experiment alive through the slow start (S003).
2. **An idle mango factory.** Zoho bought an abandoned mango pulp factory and its land about 8 or 9 km outside Tenkasi and made it a software center. Lesson: an idle factory is a cheap place to start (S003).
3. **The $25 million offer.** A buyer offered $25 million when the company had about $2 million in revenue. The partners voted unanimously to keep the company. "They offered us $25 million. We only had $2 million revenue" (S004@22:13). Lesson: keep decisions collective, and do not sell in a bubble (S001, S004).
4. **The customer who called him an idiot.** A Silicon Valley customer told him he was an idiot for underpricing. Lesson: the first job of an entrepreneur is learning to sell (S001).
5. **The Silicon Valley salesman.** A salesman told him to keep quiet and let the seller close. "You keep quiet. I'll handle this. You watch." (S004@15:58). Lesson: watch a good seller before you sell yourself (S004).
6. **The sales hire who tripled revenue.** After a sales hire, revenue went from about $2 million to $8 million in a year (S004). Lesson: a proper seller changes the numbers (S004).
7. **Japanese printer makers.** Japanese printer makers bought his network software because their main rival, HP, had network management software they lacked. Lesson: find the gap that your customers' competitors cannot fill (S001).
8. **A fab in Chandigarh.** India had a semiconductor fab in Chandigarh before Taiwan had one. The country did not build on that lead and moved into IT services instead. Lesson: a first-mover lead is lost when a country picks services over products (S005).
9. **The journalist and the word exit.** A journalist asked what exit meant. He said it meant death. Lesson: build a company to be held, not sold (S005).
10. **The 2000 term sheet.** A VC offered a good valuation with a seven-year liquidity clause, and he refused it. Lesson: align the money with your goals (S008).
11. **Writely and the move to CRM.** Zoho launched a cloud word processor, and Google bought Writely six months later. Zoho moved toward CRM, where it judged it could beat Salesforce. Lesson: when a giant enters your space, move to a niche it does not cover (S008).
12. **The 1996 network product.** A product from 1996 still brings in a couple of million dollars a year. Lesson: a small niche can fund the rest (S008).
13. **Zoho University.** It started with six children more than a decade earlier. By 2017 it had 65 to 70 students in Chennai and 30 to 35 in Tenkasi, and about 15 percent of the workforce came through it. By 2018 it took about 150 students a year (S006, S008). Lesson: test a hiring idea on a small group first, and non-degree talent can be trained.
14. **The SaaS division.** The SaaS division took six or seven years to produce meaningful revenue, funded by installed-software products. The first real revenue came around 2011 or 2012 (S012). Lesson: fund a long bet with the products that already pay.
15. **Koo.** An app with government backing could not manage user-generated content, and it shut down (S012). Lesson: B2C needs a strong foundation or a lot of capital.
16. **UPI.** About 20 years ago, US players said fees below 2 or 3 percent could not be done. India now handles about half of the world's digital transactions (S009). Lesson: choose to be cheaper and better, not bigger.
17. **The Japanese lens maker.** A Japanese lens maker does not sell the grinding machines it uses (S009). Lesson: make the tools that make the product.
18. **Mango prices crash.** Mango prices fell to five or six rupees a kilogram after overproduction (S010). Lesson: software can crash the same way when supply outruns demand.
19. **The customer who wanted protection from his own government.** In 2013 an American customer asked for protection from their own government. He said no company can give that (S011). Lesson: honest limits are better than false promises.
